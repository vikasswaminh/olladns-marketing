DNS Over HTTPS (DoH): The Protocol That's Quietly Solving One of DNS's Oldest Problems
Imagine if every time you typed a web address into your browser, someone was standing outside your window watching you do it. They couldn't see what you were reading once the page loaded—your browser's encryption handled but they could see every single domain name you requested. Your banking site. Your health insurance portal. That political news site you checked at midnight. The dating app you prefer to keep private. Your email account. Every single lookup, visible to anyone positioned between you and your DNS resolver.
That used to be the reality of how DNS worked. It still is, for most of the world.
DNS Over HTTPS (DoH) is the protocol quietly fixing that. And while it's been around for a few years now, most people still don't realize it's even possible to encrypt DNS traffic at all, let alone how much of a difference it makes to both security and privacy the moment you understand what's happening beneath the surface.

Why DNS Visibility Became Dangerous
Here's something that might surprise you: your Internet Service Provider can see every single domain you visit. So can anyone run a DNS resolver you use. So can someone with network access between your device and that resolver—a malicious actor on your home WiFi, a compromised router, a nation-state running a network tap on an undersea cable. They all have the same view.
The reason is brutally simple: DNS traffic, until DoH came along, traveled in plaintext.
When you typed "bankofamerica.com" into your browser, a DNS query went out across the network in a format that is readable to anyone paying attention. Your device asked: "Hey, where is bankofamerica.com located?" That question traveled unencrypted through routers, ISP infrastructure, and potentially through third-party DNS resolvers, and anyone with basic network monitoring tools could see it.
This isn't paranoia. This is documented, routine practice. ISPs have been caught selling DNS query data. Authoritarian governments have weaponized DNS visibility to identify and suppress dissidents. Advertisers have purchased DNS data to build impossibly detailed profiles of user behavior. Hostile actors have used DNS visibility to identify which security tools users were installing by watching them query security vendor domains.
This is the environment where DoH emerged. And to understand why it matters, you need to sit with what DNS visibility means in practice.
Think about what your DNS queries reveal about you.
The domains you visit before you log into them tell a story. That query for "linkedin.com" at 9 AM—you're potentially looking for a new job. That string of queries for divorce attorneys, medical conditions, medication names, or therapy services—someone watching your DNS traffic learns something deeply personal, whether you follow through on any of those searches. Someone monitoring your DNS could tell that you're pregnant (OB/GYN queries) before you've told anyone. They could tell you're struggling with addiction (rehab facility queries), or that you're questioning your sexual orientation (LGBTQ+ support queries), or that you're planning to leave an abusive relationship (domestic violence resources). DNS visibility is behavioral exposure at the most intimate level.
And yet, for decades, this was just how DNS worked. Full stop. No encryption. No privacy. Just a protocol from 1982, never designed to withstand the kind of surveillance that became routine in the 2010s.
DNS Over HTTPS fixed that. Not perfectly—there are complications—but significantly.

What DNS Over HTTPS Actually Does (And doesn't)
Let's start with the technical fundamentals, because "wrapping DNS in HTTPS" sounds simple but involves some genuinely important nuances.
Traditional DNS queries work like this:
  1.Your device asks for its configured resolver (often provided by your ISP, or a public one like 8.8.8.8): "What's the IP address for bankofamerica.com?"
  2.That query travels to the resolver in plaintext on UDP port 53
  3.The resolver looks up the answer and sends it back, also in plain text.
  4.Anyone monitoring traffic on the path sees both the query and the response
DNS Over HTTPS short-circuits this. It says: "Instead of sending that DNS query in plain text, let's wrap it inside a regular HTTPS request."

Here's what happens with DoH:
  1.Your device encrypts a DNS query using the normal DNS protocol
  2.That encrypted query gets bundled inside an HTTPS POST request to a DoH-supporting resolver
  3.From the perspective of anyone monitoring your network traffic, it just looks like you're browsing the web—it's indistinguishable from normal HTTPS traffic
  4.The resolver processes the query and sends back a response, also wrapped in HTTPS, also indistinguishable from regular web browsing
  5.Your device unwraps the response and uses the answer
The result: the contents of your DNS queries remain visible only to you and the resolver you've chosen to trust.

But here's the critical part that often gets glossed over in explanations: DoH doesn't stop the resolver you're using from seeing your queries.
This matters. A lot of the privacy marketing around DoH can sound like it makes DNS queries invisible to everyone, which isn't quite accurate. What it does is make your queries visible only to the DoH resolver you've configured to use, and to whoever is monitoring the path between you and that resolver. It removes visibility from your ISP, your network administrator, any tool sitting on your home Wi-Fi, anyone routing your traffic. But the resolver still knows.
This is why choosing your DoH resolver is an important decision, and why some DoH services are backed by privacy-first organizations (Mozilla and Nextdns, Cloudflare's privacy-focused offerings, Quad9), while others are backed by companies whose business models depend heavily on knowing what you browse (some ISPs now offer DoH specifically to keep you from switching to a competitor's resolver).
The second thing worth understanding is that while DoH encrypts the contents of queries, it doesn't necessarily hide the fact that DNS queries are happening or how frequently they happen. Advanced traffic analysis can sometimes infer which domain is being requested based on query size, timing, and patterns—if someone knows you're visiting one of three possible sites and can see that a specific-sized DNS query happens every 15 minutes, they might infer which one. This is called "DNS-level traffic analysis," it's not trivial to perform at scale, but it's a real limitation to DoH, not a complete privacy guarantee.
Most importantly: DoH is encryption in transit. It protects your queries while they're moving across the network. It does nothing to protect them once they arrive at the resolver. An untrustworthy resolver can still see everything, log into everything, and do whatever it wants with that data. DoH doesn't change that; it just shifts to who can see your traffic.
This distinction matters more than marketing usually makes it sound.

How DoH Differs from DoT and DoQ
DoH didn't arrive alone. There are three competing encrypted DNS protocols, and understanding the differences matters for deployment and for thinking about what "encrypted DNS" means in practice.

DNS Over TLS (DoT)
DoT works similarly to DoH but uses a dedicated TLS connection on port 853 (or 443, or any port you configure—but traditionally 853). Instead of wrapping DNS queries inside HTTPS traffic, DoT establishes a secure TLS connection specifically for DNS traffic.
The technical advantage: it's cleaner from a network standpoint. A network administrator can see that DoT traffic is happening and manage it separately from web traffic if they want to.
The practical disadvantage: because DoT uses a dedicated port, it's distinguishable from normal web browsing. A network administrator can see that DoT traffic is happening and potentially block it outright. A censoring regime can block an entire port known to carry encrypted DNS. DoT is more transparent and more predictable, but also more block able.

DNS Over QUIC (DoQ)
DoQ is the newest of the three, built on QUIC transport protocol (the protocol that underpins HTTP/3). It combines some of the benefits of the others: faster connection establishment than traditional TLS, built on a newer transport designed for modern networks, and the ability to blend with normal HTTPS traffic since QUIC and HTTPS/3 are often indistinguishable to a casual observer.
The catch: DoQ is newer, less widely deployed, and requires devices and resolvers to support QUIC, which still isn't universal.

DoH	443 (HTTPS)	Yes—perfectly	Slower initial	No—looks like web	Very difficult
DoT	853 (or 443)	No—dedicated port	Moderate	Yes—distinctive traffic	Easy with port rules
DoQ	443 (QUIC)	Mostly—blends with HTTP3	Faster	Difficult—newer	Harder than DoT

DoH Won the Visibility Battle, But for Complicated Reasons
DoH has become the de facto standard for consumer encrypted DNS, not because it's technically superior—DoT has some legitimate advantages—but because of how it behaves at the network level. By blending with normal web traffic, DoH is nearly impossible to distinguish from regular browsing, which means:
  •Network administrators can't easily block it without blocking all HTTPS traffic
  •Censoring regimes can't target it specifically without broader internet disruption
  •It works seamlessly over any network that allows HTTPS, including hotel WiFi, coffee shops, and censored countries
That invisibility, ironically, is why major tech companies (Apple, Mozilla, Google) made DoH their default. It bypasses network filtering, which can be a genuine privacy feature in hostile environments but also means it bypasses legitimate network security controls at the same time.
This is one of the interesting tensions in DoH deployment that we'll dig into more in a moment.

Privacy Win and the Security Blind Spot
This is where the story gets genuinely complicated, because DoH solves some serious privacy problems but creates others.

The Privacy Win
DoH stops your ISP from collecting and potentially selling your DNS query history. That's real. That matters. An ISP that can't see your queries can't build a profile of your behavior, can't sell that data to advertisers, can't be compelled by subpoena to hand over a list of every site you visited, can't silently redirect you if you mistype a domain.
This is particularly important in certain geographies. In countries with heavy internet surveillance, DNS visibility is a direct pathway to identifying dissident activity. Organizations like Amnesty International have documented how governments use DNS logs to identify journalists and political activists. For those populations, switching to encrypted DNS with a trusted provider can be a meaningful safety measure.
The ISP-level privacy win of DoH is real and nontrivial.

The Security Complication
But here's what happens when everyone starts using DoH: it becomes much harder for legitimate network security to do their job.
A school network trying to block adult content can't inspect DoH queries the same way they could inspect plaintext DNS. A hospital trying to ensure HIPAA compliance by restricting certain infrastructure queries gets blind spots. A corporate network trying to block command-and-control beaconing from malware can't see DoH queries leaving an infected device.
This isn't a theoretical problem. Real organizations with legitimate reasons to monitor DNS traffic—compliance requirements, security operations, protecting minors, infrastructure protection—are suddenly finding that a privacy-enhancing protocol is also making their own security invisible to their own tools.
The tension between privacy and security is real here, and it's not a tension that has a clean resolution.
Different organizations are handling this differently:
  •Some are moving to DoH at the network level, using a DoH resolver they control internally, so the encryption still happens (protecting queries from external eavesdropping) but internal security monitoring can still function
  •Some are deploying endpoint controls that inspect queries before they're encrypted (defeating the privacy benefit from the user's perspective, but allowing monitoring)
  •Some are accepting that encrypted DNS means they've lost that visibility and compensation with other monitoring techniques
  •Some organizations are actively fighting DoH adoption, or configuring networks to intercept and block it
This is one of the major reasons why DoH hasn't been universally adopted as quickly as pure encryption advocates might have expected. There are genuine, legitimate reasons—not just privacy hostility—why some organizations are slow to embrace it.

Why "Just Enable DoH" Isn't the Whole Story
Most operating systems now support DoH natively. Most browsers support it. If you're reading this on a modern device, you probably have the capability to enable encrypted DNS right now. And there are still a hundred reasons why most people and most organizations haven't.

Discovery and Configuration
First, the barrier to entry. DoH isn't "on by default" in the way that HTTPS became on by default. You must actively configure it. On most systems, you need to:
  1.Know that DoH exists
  2.Know how to enable it on your specific device or browser
  3.Choose which DoH resolver to use
  4.Configure it correctly
  5.Verify it's working
For the average user, especially non-technical ones, this is friction. Real, meaningful friction. Compare this to HTTPS, which became the web default essentially invisibly—websites just started requiring it, browsers started supporting it, users never had to make a choice or understand what was happening.
DoH still requires active decision-making.

Resolver Selection Isn't Neutral
This is the part that privacy advocates often skip over too quickly: choosing a DoH resolver is a high-trust decision.
You're not just choosing a service that encrypts your traffic in transit. You're choosing an organization to see all your DNS queries. That resolver knows everything you search for, everywhere you go online. They know when you do it, how frequently, in what patterns. They have behavioral data on you that most organizations would kill to have.
So, the resolver you choose matters enormously. Using a DoH service from a company whose business model depends on advertising profiling is better than letting your ISP see your queries, but not by as much as you'd hoped. You're trading one form of visibility for another.
This is why privacy-focused organizations have positioned themselves as DoH providers: Nextdns, Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 for Families, Quad9. They're explicitly designed around not profiling users. They often delete logs after very short periods. But you must know that choice exists, and you must actively switch to it.

DoH Creates New Network Configuration Problems
Here's a practical problem that doesn't get discussed much: DoH bypasses DNS configured at the network level.
In an organization, the IT team configures a DNS resolver for everyone to use. That resolver might be internal, might be customized, might have organizational policy baked in. When an employee's device is on corporate WiFi, everything works as intended.
Then that employee goes home on a weekend and enables DoH with a public resolver. Now their device stops using the corporate DNS resolver entirely and starts querying a resolver in the cloud. The organization has lost visibility and control. The employee has gained privacy. From a security perspective, it's complicated.
This is one of the reasons why IT teams have historically been skeptical of DoH. It's not hostility to privacy; it's that it breaks assumptions about how network security and monitoring work.
Sophisticated organizations are handling this with mobile device management that pushes custom certificate authorities and DoH resolver configurations to devices, but that's a level of complexity most organizations don't have.

ISP Resistance and Weaponization Concerns
There's a genuinely contentious political element to DoH adoption. Some ISPs have fought it explicitly, arguing (not always unfairly) that it prevents them from offering network services that depend on DNS visibility—parental controls, malware blocking, compliance monitoring.
Others have seen DoH as a threat to their business models, where DNS data is a saleable commodity. A few have fought it legislatively, arguing that networks need to be able to inspect traffic for security reasons.
On the flip side, some organizations have also weaponized DoH fears. There was a period where some corporate networks were actively blocking DoH on principle, arguing that "employees shouldn't have to hide their activities." Which is... a concerning argument about surveillance, but it's an argument that gets made.
The point: DoH's adoption friction isn't just technical. It's political. Different organizations and different governments view encrypted DNS very differently.

How Encryption Actually Protects DNS
To understand what DoH protects you against, it's worth being specific about the attack vectors it does and doesn't address.

Man-in-the-Middle Attacks
This is the classic threat DoH directly prevents. Someone sitting on the path between you and your resolver—a hostile WiFi network, a compromised router, a network tap—can no longer intercept and modify your DNS traffic.
Without encryption: attacker intercepts your query for "bankofamerica.com," responds with a fake IP pointing to a malicious server. You type what you think is the right URL but end up somewhere else.
With DoH: the query is encrypted and travels inside normal HTTPS traffic, indistinguishable from regular web browsing. An attacker still sees that HTTPS traffic is happening but can't modify the contents of that query or inject a fake response. The worst they can do is block the request entirely, not redirect it.

ISP-Level Surveillance
Plaintext DNS leaks everything to your ISP. They see every domain you query, often in real time, with detailed logs showing IP address, timestamp, the exact query, the answer.
DoH stops that completely. The ISP can see that you're communicating with a DoH resolver (they can see the IP address of the resolver), and they can see the volume of traffic, but they can't see what domains you're querying.
This eliminates an entire category of behavioral tracking that used to be routine.

Government-Level DNS Data Collection
Some governments have compelled ISPs to hand over DNS logs as a form of mass surveillance. In the US, this has been done under various legal authorities; in other countries, it's standard practice. DoH makes that data collection impossible if the resolver is outside the country and resists requests.
This is particularly meaningful for people living under surveillance regimes or fleeing abusive situations, where DNS visibility could be literally dangerous.

Censorship Circumvention
In countries with heavy internet filtering and censorship, the government often controls DNS at the infrastructure level—both through ISP mandates and through network-level filtering. If you query for a censored domain, your ISP's resolver just returns an error or a blocked response.
DoH to a resolver outside the country bypasses that entirely. You query a foreign resolver, get the real answer, and can attempt to connect. This is why censoring regimes often try to block DoH specifically—it's one of the more effective lightweight circumvention tools available.
This is genuine, consequential stuff.

What DoH Doesn't Protect Against
But there are important attacks that encryption doesn't prevent:

Traffic Analysis - As mentioned earlier, the size of DNS queries and the timing patterns can sometimes leak information. If an observer knows you're visiting one of three possible sites and can see the query size and timing, they might infer which one. This is rare and requires sophisticated analysis, but it's a real limit to DoH's privacy.

Malicious Resolvers - If the DoH resolver you're using is malicious or compromised, they see everything. Encryption only protects you from observers on the network path, not from the resolver itself.

Application-Layer Leaks - Even if DNS is encrypted, you might leak your browsing behavior through other channels. Your browser might request a domain in its search suggestions. Your email client might send device names. Your apps might phone home with unencrypted identifiers. DoH solves DNS; it doesn't solve all privacy.

Endpoint Compromise - If your device is malware-infected or compromised, none of the encryption in the world matters. The malware can see queries before they're encrypted.

Metadata - Even if the resolver can't see your queries, they can see your IP address, the volume of traffic, and the timing. That's still behavioral data.

DoH is powerful and important. But it's not magic. It solves a specific problem—DNS query privacy in transit—and does so very well. It doesn't solve all privacy problems, and privacy advocates sometimes overstate what it provides.

Deployment Realities: How Organizations Actually Implement DoH
If you're running a technology team and trying to figure out DoH deployment in practice, the clean theoretical picture gets muddy fast.

For Individual Users: Easy, But Confusing
If you're just an individual wanting to use DoH, the process is simple:
Browser level: Firefox and Chrome both support DoH. You can enable it in settings within minutes. Just pick your resolver and turn it on.


Operating System level: Windows 11, macOS (Monterey and later), iOS (14.7+), and Android (9+) all support DoH. You can configure it at the system level so that all apps benefit from it, not just the browser.

Device level: VPN apps, dedicated DNS apps, and security tools also support DoH configuration.
The barrier isn't capability—it's awareness and decision-making. Most people don't know it's an option. Of those who do, many are unsure which resolver to choose, and making the wrong choice (trusting a malicious resolver, or one that logs aggressively) can be worse than using plaintext DNS.

For Organizations: Friction Everywhere
Deploying DoH at the organizational level is where the complexity shows up.
The Problem with Bypassing Network Security
The moment an employee enables DoH with an external resolver, the organization's internal DNS security infrastructure stops working. If you've configured your network resolver to block malicious domains, and an employee's device starts using a DoH resolver in the cloud, they've silently bypassed that protection.
This is solved by deploying a managed DoH resolver within the organization—basically, standing up your own DoH-supporting resolver internally, with organizational policy baked in—but this requires:
  •Infrastructure to run the resolver
  •Management of TLS certificates
  •Ensuring the resolver is fast and available
  •Monitoring and logging
  •Technical sophistication to deploy correctly
Many organizations simply don't have this capability, so they're stuck choosing between allowing the privacy benefit (but losing DNS-layer security visibility) or actively blocking DoH (and losing the privacy benefit).

The Certificate Authority Problem
Some organizations intercept HTTPS traffic at the network level for security monitoring. This requires installing a corporate certificate authority on employees' devices, which allows the network to decrypt HTTPS traffic (a controversial practice, but done for legitimate compliance reasons in some industries).
This creates an interesting collision with DoH. When an employee's device has a corporate CA installed and tries to use DoH, the organization can intercept and inspect the encrypted DNS. But if the employee uses a public DoH resolver that explicitly resists such interception (Cloudflare, Nextdns), they might be able to bypass it.
The result is ongoing tension between organizational security requirements and user privacy expectations.

MDM Complexity
Properly deploying DoH at scale requires Mobile Device Management (Intune, Jamf, etc.) to push configurations to devices. This works fine if your organization has MDM, but a lot of organizations, especially smaller ones or those in certain industries, don't have comprehensive device management.

Performance Question
Here's a practical one: does encrypted DNS work well enough to deploy at scale?
The answer is yes, mostly. Modern DoH resolvers are fast—typically responding in 50-200 milliseconds, which is faster than many ISP resolvers. There's a theoretical performance hit from the HTTPS overhead, but in practice, most users don't notice it.
The exception is on lossy, high-latency networks where the traditional UDP-based plaintext DNS is faster than HTTPS with its TCP/TLS handshake. But for most modern networks, DoH is fine.

The Filtering Complication
Organizations that filter DNS based on categories (blocking gambling, adult content, social media, etc.) suddenly can't do that if employees use external DoH resolvers.
This matters for:
  •Schools filtering for CIPA compliance
  •Enterprises trying to improve productivity by blocking social media during work hours
  •Parents trying to protect children with device-level filtering
  •Compliance-heavy industries where data exfiltration prevention requires network-layer controls
All of these become much harder with DoH. Some organizations are responding by implementing endpoint-based solutions (blocking before encryption happens), but that moves the locus of control and requires more infrastructure.

The Standards-Based Future: What's Actually Shipping
DoH has been an RFC (RFC 8484) since 2018, which means it's officially a standard. But the practical rollout has been messy because different organizations have different priorities.

Where DoH Is Default Now
Browsers: Firefox ships with DoH enabled by default (in the US; it's configurable in other regions). Chrome is moving toward it gradually. Safari and Edge support it but often default to ISP resolvers. Brave, Opera, and other browsers have it enabled by default.
Operating Systems: macOS, Windows 11, iOS, and recent Android versions all have native DoH support, though it's not always enabled by default.
Mobile: iOS and Android both support DoH, and more apps are starting to use it automatically.
ISPs: A few forward-thinking ISPs are offering DoH services directly, though most haven't.
Enterprise: Some enterprise security platforms now support DoH, but adoption is still patchy.

The Adoption Curve
The general trend is positive. More devices support it. More resolvers offer it. More organizations are deploying it. But adoption is still far from universal.
A reasonable estimate is that somewhere between 20-35% of internet users have DoH enabled in some form—mostly in developed countries, mostly on mobile devices and browsers, less on enterprise networks.

What's Still Missing
For DoH to become truly universal, a few things would need to happen:
Better discovery: Users need an easier way to know which resolver to use. Right now, it's mostly word-of-mouth and tech blogs. There should be something like a "show me reputable DoH providers" interface in operating systems.
ISP adoption: If ISPs provided DoH resolvers by default, deployment would accelerate dramatically. Some are, but most aren't.
Enterprise integration: Organizations need DoH to integrate more seamlessly with their existing security, monitoring, and management infrastructure. This is getting better but isn't there yet.
Performance guarantees: While DoH is fast enough in practice, there's no standardized way to guarantee SLAs or measure performance across different resolvers, which matters for organizations.
Standardized policy: Right now, organizations wanting to deploy DoH internally must build a lot of it from scratch. Having standardized configuration and policy templates would help.

A Real-World Deployment Checklist
If your organization is considering DoH, here's what needs to happen:

1. Define Your Threat Model
Are you trying to protect against ISP surveillance? Network eavesdropping? Internal user privacy? Compliance requirements? DoH solves some problems, not all. Be clear about what problem you're solving.

2. Understand Your Constraints
Do you have compliance requirements that mandate network-level DNS filtering? Do you need to block certain categories of content? Do you have mobile device management? Do you need to monitor DNS for security incidents? Each constraint affects the solution.

3. Choose a Resolution Strategy
Three basic approaches:
  •Managed provider: Use a DoH resolver from a third party (Cloudflare, Nextdns, Quad9, etc.). Simple to deploy. Lowest control. Requires trusting the provider.
  •Internal resolver: Stand up your own DoH resolver internally. More complex. Full control. Requires infrastructure and maintenance.
  •Hybrid: Use internal resolver for organizational traffic, allow external for personal. Most complex. Most control. Hardest to manage.

4. Deploy at the Right Layers
  •Browser level: Easiest to enable, most limited (doesn't cover all applications)
  •OS level: Covers all traffic from that device
  •Network level: Covers everything connecting through your network
  •MDM level: Deploy via mobile device management for centralized control

5. Plan for Exceptions
DoH will block legitimate traffic at some point. Have a clear exception process. Make it fast, or users will find workarounds.

6. Measure What Matters
  •Adoption rate: Are people using DoH?
  •Performance: Any degradation?
  •Privacy: Is it reducing ISP-level surveillance?
  •Security: Are you still catching threats on the DNS layer?

7. Plan for Maintenance
DoH isn't set-and-forget. You need:
  •Certificate management
  •Monitoring and alerting
  •Security updates
  •Configuration reviews
  •User support

The Reality of DoH in Hostile Environments
Most discussions of DoH assume a somewhat neutral network environment. But in censored countries and hostile networks, DoH looks very different.

The Circumvention Value
For someone living in a country with heavy internet filtering, DoH to a resolver outside the country can be genuinely life changing. It can make censored information accessible. It can prevent governments from tracking which forbidden websites people access.
This is why censoring regimes often try to block DoH specifically. Some countries are now implementing Trickbot tactics—blocking known DoH resolver IPs, monitoring for the distinctive traffic patterns of encrypted DNS, even in some cases blocking entire ports or protocols associated with DoH.
This has led to an arms race where privacy advocates are working on DoH techniques that are even harder to detect and block.

The Organizational Firewall Problem
Conversely, if an organization is trying to operate in a heavily censored country and needs to use local infrastructure, DoH that bypasses all filters can be a problem. An employee using DoH with an external resolver could accidentally access blocked content that gets them and the organization in legal trouble.
Different organizations have handled this differently—some embrace DoH for privacy, others actively block it.

Common DoH Misconceptions
"DoH Makes Me Completely Anonymous"
Nope. It makes your queries private in transit, but the resolver still knows who you are (your IP address), and anyone analyzing traffic patterns might still infer behavior. Plus, your browsing behavior leaks through dozens of other channels (HTTP headers, TLS certificates with SNI, etc.).

"DoH Is Slower Than Regular DNS"
No longer true in practice. Modern DoH resolvers are often faster than ISP resolvers, especially ISP resolvers in underserved areas.

"DoH Breaks Network Security"
It complicates it, specifically DNS-layer security. But it doesn't make security impossible; it just requires rethinking the architecture.

"I Don't Have Anything to Hide"
This is never the right way to think about privacy. Privacy isn't about hiding wrongdoing; it's about autonomy. You don't need to hide to want your browsing behavior kept private. Plus, behavioral data has real economic value and enables real harm (discrimination, profiling, manipulation).

"DoH Is the Same as a VPN"
No. A VPN encrypts all traffic and hides your IP address. DoH only encrypts DNS and doesn't hide your IP. A VPN is more comprehensive privacy protection. DoH is more focused.

"DoH Stops Malware"
No. Encrypted DNS doesn't protect you from malware on your device or from connecting to malicious servers if you get the IP address. It just stops someone from seeing your DNS queries.

The Honest Take: What DoH Is and Isn't
Here's the practical reality:

What DoH actually solves:
  •Stops ISP-level surveillance of your queries
  •Prevents network-level eavesdropping and tampering
  •Helps circumvent DNS-based censorship
  •Protects you from ISPs selling your data

What DoH doesn't solve:
  •Malicious resolvers (you have to trust the resolver you choose)
  •Endpoint compromise (if your device is infected, encryption doesn't help)
  •Application-layer leaks (other channels can still leak behavior)
  •All forms of surveillance (traffic analysis and metadata leaks remain)
  •Network-level security filtering (organizations lose that)

When DoH makes sense:
  •You're concerned about ISP surveillance
  •You're in a country with heavy censorship
  •You want better privacy on public WiFi
  •You're using a privacy-conscious resolver
  •You're on a modern device that supports it well

When DoH complicates things:
  •You need organizational DNS filtering
  •You have compliance requirements
  •You're running an older network with legacy systems
  •You want to maintain centralized network monitoring
  •You need to monitor DNS for security incidents

The honest version is this: DoH is a genuinely important privacy-enhancing technology that solves a real problem (ISP-level DNS surveillance). It's not magic. It doesn't solve all privacy problems. It does introduce some operational complexities for organizations. But as a piece of a privacy-focused strategy, it's solid.
The internet needs encrypted DNS. DoH delivered it. And we're still working through what that means for everyone.

Frequently Asked Question's

Does enabling DoH mean my ISP can't see my traffic?
Correct. Your ISP can't see which domains you're querying once you're using DoH. They can see that you're communicating with a DoH resolver (based on IP address and traffic volume), but not the contents of your queries.

Does DoH encrypt my internet connection?
No. DoH only encrypts DNS queries. Your other traffic still depends on individual sites using HTTPS. DoH is just the DNS part.

Can my employer see my DoH queries if I'm on corporate WiFi?
Depends on their setup. If they've deployed a managed DoH resolver internally, they can see queries. If you're using an external DoH resolver, they might not be able to see the queries themselves, but they can see you're using an external resolver and might block it. It's complicated.

Is DoH faster than regular DNS?
Usually yes, or at least at the same speed. Modern DoH resolvers typically respond faster than ISP resolvers. There's theoretically more overhead from HTTPS, but in practice it's faster.

Which DoH resolver should I use?
Depends on what you prioritize:
  •Privacy: Nextdns, Quad9, or Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 for Families
  •Performance: Cloudflare, Google DNS, Quad9 (all fast)
  •Filtering: Depends on your needs; research what's available
  •Internal: Your organization's resolver if you're on a corporate network

Does DoH work on all devices?
Mostly yes. Modern operating systems and browsers support it. Older devices might not. Specialized devices (some routers, IoT, older systems) often don't.

Can my ISP block DoH?
Technically yes, but it's difficult. DoH traffic looks like normal HTTPS, so blocking it would require blocking large portions of the internet. Some ISPs in censored countries try, but it's not simple.

Does DoH stop malware?
No. Encryption doesn't protect you from malware on your device. It just stops someone from seeing your queries. Malware can still infect your system and behave badly.

Is DoH the same as DNSSEC?
No. DNSSEC verifies that DNS responses come from legitimate sources (authentication). DoH encrypts queries in transit (privacy). They solve different problems and work together.

Should I always use DoH?
If you value privacy and your device supports it well, yes. If you have organizational requirements that need DNS visibility, it's more complicated. Most experts agree that DoH should be the default, with opt-outs for specific use cases.

The Bottom Line: Small Change, Big Impact
DNS Over HTTPS is a technology that works quietly in the background. Most people using it don't think about it. But it represents something important: the recognition that a 40-year-old protocol designed before surveillance was a concern needed updating for a world where privacy can't be assumed.
DoH doesn't solve everything. It doesn't stop sophisticated attacks, doesn't protect against malware, doesn't make you completely anonymous. But it does something that matters, it stops your ISP from knowing everywhere you go online. It stops a basic form of surveillance that used to be completely routine.
That's worth something. More than worth something. It's the kind of foundational infrastructure change that doesn't make headlines but makes the internet a little bit less hostile to privacy.
The fact that it's still not default everywhere is mostly inertia—technical organizations slow to change, ISPs resisting visibility loss, organizations trying to maintain filtering capabilities. But the direction is clear. DoH is becoming baseline. Not immediately, not everywhere, but gradually, the web is moving toward encrypted DNS as standard.
And in a world where your DNS queries used to be about as private as reading your browser history in the airport, that shift matters.
